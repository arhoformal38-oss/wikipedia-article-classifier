Good morning. We are Dina and Rand. Our project takes the abstract of a Wikipedia article and decides what kind of thing it describes: a company, an artist, an athlete, a building, a film or a written work. We used corpus option 6, DBpedia-14.
The problem

One abstract in, one type out

Input: the first paragraph of a Wikipedia article. Output: one of six types. The type is never written as a label — it has to be read from how the article is written.

🏢 Company🎨 Artist⚽ Athlete 🏛️ Building🎬 Film📖 WrittenWork
Easy
“Gibraltar is a 1938 French drama film directed by Fedor Ozep and starring …”
→ Film. The phrasing gives it away.
Hard
“Jonathan Rosenbaum is an American film critic …”
→ Artist (a person), but the text is full of film.
Why it matters: search filters, knowledge bases and automatic tagging of new pages all need to know the type of an article first.
This is text classification with six classes. Some articles are easy because Wikipedia uses fixed phrases like “directed by”. Others are hard: a film critic is a person, but the article talks about films all the time. Keep this example in mind — it comes back in the error analysis.

DBpedia-14 has 560,000 training and 70,000 test articles. We kept six classes. Training uses 3,000 random articles per class; the test set is the full official test split for those classes, 30,000 articles. The test set was saved before any modelling and only used once at the very end.

We counted unigrams, bigrams and trigrams per class and ranked phrases that are frequent in one class but rare in the others. “Film directed” appears 1,452 times in film abstracts. Shared phrases like “united states” or “born in March” carry little type information.

Same model: worst with add-k, best with interpolation. Weights chosen on validation: 0.2 · tri + 0.5 · bi + 0.3 · uni.
The trigram model learned Wikipedia’s style. The interesting result is smoothing: with add-k the trigram was the worst model, because most three-word contexts are rare and add-k gives too much probability to unseen words. With interpolation it became the best. This is the sparsity problem from Unit 3.

Error analysis

What the model learned — and where it fails

Strongest words per class
paintersingerfootballerplayermuseumchurchmanufacturerfoundeddirecteddocumentarynovelnewspaper
Bag of Words counts words but cannot tell what an article is about from what it mentions.
Mentions ≠ is about: writers are described through their books → WrittenWork (145 artists).
Two types at once: hotels, shops and factories are businesses and places → Company ↔ Building.
Wrong labels: Wea, a Native American tribe, is labelled Company in DBpedia.
It knows when it is unsure: confidence 0.97 when right, 0.72 when wrong.
The words with the largest weights match the N-gram tables. Errors come from three sources: articles that mention one type but describe another, entities that really are two types, and a few wrong labels in the dataset. Wrong predictions have much lower confidence, so they could be flagged.
